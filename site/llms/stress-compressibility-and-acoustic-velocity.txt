## Measurement of Pore Compressibility Characteristics in Rock Exhibiting "Pore Collapse" and Volumetric Creep

- ID: SCA1991-24, year: 1991
- Authors: J. M. Hamilton, J. L. Shafer
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: pore collapse, volumetric creep, pore compressibility, reservoir compaction, loading rate correction, diatomite
- Cited by 2 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1991/1-SCA1991-24.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1991-24)

Hydrostatic compaction and creep tests on diatomite and carbonate show fast lab loading overestimates pore-collapse stress; a log-rate extrapolation corrects compaction predictions to field depletion rates.

Standard lab pore compressibility tests load rocks at hundreds of psi/hr, versus about 0.01 psi/hr in the field, so rocks that creep show an apparent preconsolidation and an overestimated pore-collapse stress. Hydrostatic tests with tetradecane as pore fluid were run on a diatomite and a moldic carbonate, varying loading rate from 1000 to 10 psi/hr and including a 10-day constant-stress creep hold at 1500 psi. Creep was linear in void ratio versus log time, about 0.05 per log cycle for diatomite, matching the separation between loading-rate curves. Extrapolating five log cycles lowers the diatomite yield estimate by about 250 psi and the carbonate by about 1300 psi, and in a hypothetical 200 ft diatomite example raises predicted compaction from 4 ft to 20 ft. Incomplete pore pressure dissipation, seen as an overshoot after yield, can mimic rate effects; diatomite matrix permeability (0.003 md) was far below flow-through permeability (0.05 md), requiring about two hours between load steps.

## Laboratory Measurement of Oil/Water Saturations by an Ultrasonic Technique

- ID: SCA1992-11EURO, year: 1992
- Authors: F. Deflandre, R. Lenormand
- Method: measurement technique or apparatus
- Keywords: sound velocity, saturation measurement, ultrasonic transit time, imbibition calibration, temperature effect, Biot theory
- Cited by 2 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1992/1-SCA1992-11EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1992-11EURO)

Ultrasonic sound-velocity calibration for oil/water saturation in cores during imbibition is linear for So 0.2-0.5, can use effluent mass balance, but is highly temperature sensitive.

Sound velocity through core samples is used to measure oil/water saturations during coreflooding, with calibration for immiscible fluids being the main problem. Piezoelectric transducers (about 350 kHz, 4 mm resolution, 1 ns timing) were used on Vosges and Berea sandstones and Lavoux limestone, with CT scanner (about 2% accuracy) and effluent mass balance as saturation references. For imbibition with connate oil, a linear relation between relative velocity and saturation holds for So of 0.2-0.5, and a single law fits all transducers in a homogeneous sample, so two effluent measurements can calibrate it. Temperature sensitivity is high: about +90 ns per °C in a Vosges sandstone at So=0.3, whereas pressure effect is about 35 ns per bar, mainly from the solid. The technique offers high speed, low cost and no safety hazards but is limited to liquids and needs thermal regulation.

## Mechanical Properties Analysis: The Key to Understanding Petrophysical Properties Stress Sensitivity

- ID: SCA1993-21, year: 1993
- Authors: R.A. Farquhar, B.D.G. Smart, B.R. Crawford, A.C. Todd, J.A. Tweedie
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: stress sensitivity, Young's modulus, microcracks, strain relaxation, Rotliegendes sandstone, permeability
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1993/SCA1993-21.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1993-21)

Stress sensitivity of permeability and Young's modulus in Rotliegendes sandstones depends on facies; the porosity-modulus trend fails at low stress, indicating core-recovery microcracks.

The study examines stress sensitivity of porosity, permeability, electrical conductivity and Young's modulus in low permeability Rotliegendes sandstones from three depositional facies (aeolian, fluvial, lake margin sabkha). Plugs were tested up to 34.5 MPa mean stress, with properties fitted by a dual exponential function, and elastic properties measured in a Hoek triaxial cell with strain gauges. Permeability dropped by 13% to over 90%, and Young's modulus rose by a factor of about 2 to 3.5 between 6.9 and 34.5 MPa. Stress sensitivity clustered by facies, with aeolian samples least and sabkha most sensitive in Young's modulus. The expected porosity-Young's modulus relationship developed only at high confining pressure. Anisotropy of Young's modulus, Klinkenberg slip-factor exponent (-0.37 at 6.9 MPa), and SEM/thin-section grain-boundary cracks support stress-relaxation microcracks, so low-stress routine core data may not represent in situ structure. Facies-based correction is proposed.

## Laboratory Acoustic Measurements for Reservoir Characterization: Consequences of Core Alteration

- ID: SCA1999-26, year: 1999
- Authors: Rune M. Holt
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: core damage, elastic wave velocity, stress dependence, synthetic sandstone, velocity anisotropy, stress release
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1999/SCA1999-26.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1999-26)

Synthetic sandstone made under stress and then unloaded and reloaded shows that coring stress release permanently lowers velocities by 9-12% and inflates measured stress dependence.

Core damage from stress release is usually ignored when interpreting laboratory wave velocities. Synthetic sandstone (about 20% porosity, sodium silicate cemented with CO2 under 30 MPa axial and 15 MPa radial stress) was used to simulate coring by unloading, then reloading and uniaxial compaction, with P- and S-wave velocities measured at 0.5 MHz. Cementation raised P velocities from about 2200/1995 m/s to 3460/3325 m/s, while unloading strongly reduced velocities and inverted the anisotropy. After reloading to in situ stress, velocities stayed 9-12% lower (axial P 3050 m/s). Velocities of the simulated core rose near 10% when axial stress went from 30 to 60 MPa, versus 2-3% for virgin rock, and a natural sandstone core behaved qualitatively like the simulated core. The author concludes that much of the stress dependence seen in core studies is damage-related, which can bias reservoir monitoring feasibility assessments.

## Stress Sensitivity of Saturation and End-Point Relative Permeabilities

- ID: SCA2001-11, year: 2001
- Authors: C. Jones, A. A. Al-Quraishi, J. M. Somerville, S. A. Hamilton
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: stress sensitivity, relative permeability, end-point permeability, initial water saturation, residual oil saturation, Locharbriggs sandstone
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2001/SCA2001-11.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2001-11)

Oil-water flooding of Locharbriggs sandstone at 400–8000 psi shows Swi decreases, Sor increases and oil end-point permeability falls with stress, more than single-phase permeability.

Little data exist on how multiphase flow properties respond to stress changes from pore pressure depletion. Six Locharbriggs sandstone plugs (porosity 25.4–28.0%, permeability 1051–1644 mD, strongly water wet, Amott-Harvey 0.91) were flooded to Swi and Sor in a Hoek cell, either with stress stepped up on one plug or with separate tests at 400, 3000 and 8000 psi. Saturations came from produced volumes corrected for stress-induced pore volume loss (about 5% at 8000 psi). Swi fell from about 0.40 at 400 psi to 0.385 at 3000 psi and 0.377 at 8000 psi, explained by brine squeezed from small pores. Sor rose with stress because oil volume stayed constant while pore volume shrank. Oil end-point permeability dropped about 15% from 400 to 3000 psi versus 8% for single-phase permeability, and kro at Sw=0.4 fell from 0.611 to 0.562. Water relative permeability showed no significant change.

## Interpretation and Reliability of Laboratory Tests Measuring Porosity, Pore Compressibility, and Velocity on Unconsolidated Deep Offshore Reservoirs

- ID: SCA2002-21, year: 2002
- Authors: Luc Pauget, F. Specia, A. Boubazine
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: unconsolidated sand, pore compressibility, creep logarithmic law, loading rate correction, Cam Clay model, oedometric uniaxial strain test
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 1
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2002/SCA2002-21.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2002-21)

Laboratory tests on unconsolidated reservoir sands show coring does not destroy mechanical properties; creep follows a logarithmic law enabling loading-rate correction, and isotropic tests convert to oedometric with a factor of about 0.67.

Reliable porosity and pore compressibility data on unconsolidated sands matter for deep-offshore and heavy oil reservoirs, but coring damage and viscous behavior raise doubts. The authors simulated the coring stress path in the laboratory and found that hardening is erased and initial porosity drops, yet compressibility on virgin loading and P-wave velocity are unchanged, so mechanical properties are recovered. From hundreds of creep tests lasting 1 to 30 days, a logarithmic law (De Waal) fitted better than a finite-strain power law; the creep coefficient b (about 0.024 on average) was independent of stress between 40 and 300 bar. Loading-rate correction with this coefficient matched measured compressibility over a 100-fold rate range, with corrections of 6% for a tenfold and 12% for a hundredfold change. Using Cam Clay theory, isotropic compressibility equals oedometric compressibility times (1+2K'0)/3; with K'0 of 0.45-0.55 the factor is 0.63-0.7 (about 0.67), supported by test comparisons.

## Laboratory Determination of Stress-Path Dependency of Directional Permeabilities of Estaillades Limestone

- ID: SCA2008-26, year: 2008
- Authors: J. Dautriat, N. Gland, A. Dimanov, J. Raphanel, O. Vizika
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: triaxial stress path, directional permeability, Estaillades limestone, pore collapse, yield cap, isopermeability contours
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2008/SCA2008-26.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2008-26)

Triaxial tests on Estaillades limestone along stress paths K=0 to 1 with axial and radial permeability measurements show permeability loss depends strongly on stress path, greatest under hydrostatic pore collapse.

Reservoir stress changes alter carbonate permeability and its anisotropy, but standard triaxial cells measure only axial permeability. The authors used a triaxial cell with flow ports measuring axial (full and mid-length) and two radial permeabilities on 38.1 x 80 mm Estaillades limestone plugs (porosity about 29%, k about 150 mD, bimodal pores at 10 and 0.2 µm). Samples were loaded continuously at constant K from 0 to 1. Low K gave brittle failure with dilatancy and microfracturing of dense aggregates, while high K gave pore collapse of macroporosity; hydrostatic stepwise loading showed collapse near 25-35 MPa with permeability falling to 10-20% of initial. Elliptic yield caps and a Mohr-Coulomb line were fitted, and isopermeability maps in (p',q) space show radial permeability decreasing faster than axial, largely controlled by mean stress near elastic conditions and by shear at low K.

## Pore Pressure Effects and Permeability: Effective Stress Issues for High Pressure Reservoirs

- ID: SCA2009-03, year: 2009
- Authors: Greg Boitnott, New England, Testing Analysis John Shafer
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: effective stress coefficient, permeability, pore pressure depletion, Lower Tertiary sandstone, loading path, poroelasticity
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2009/SCA2009-03.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2009-03)

Cyclic brine permeability tests on a Lower Tertiary Gulf of Mexico sandstone give an effective stress coefficient for permeability of about 2 to 2.4, and depletion paths reduce permeability more than constant-pore-pressure tests.

Routine permeability tests at reduced stress and low pore pressure may misrepresent deep, high-pressure Gulf of Mexico Lower Tertiary reservoirs. A 15 mD, 20% porosity sandstone was tested with a servo-controlled apparatus using steady-state brine flow and a protocol of three loading paths: equal changes in Pc and Pp, Pp change at constant Pc, and Pc change at constant Pp. The effective stress coefficient for permeability was 2.0–2.8 in the first test, with an average of 2.4. After repeated cycling over 5 days, permeability fell 30% and history dependence faded, giving values of 2.0–2.2. A poroelastic uniaxial-strain test showed true pore pressure depletion gave higher starting permeability and larger reduction than constant-pore-pressure loading at equivalent effective drawdown. The authors conclude that tests should use reservoir-representative stresses and pore pressures.

## Pore Volume Compressibilities under Different Stress Conditions

- ID: SCA1990-05, year: 1990
- Authors: Ziqiong Zheng, John McLennan, Arfon Jones, Terra Tek, Salt Lake City
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: pore volume compressibility, uniaxial strain, pore pressure drawdown, hydrostatic compression, pore collapse, effective stress
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1990/SCA1990-05.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1990-05)

Pore volume compressibility of sandstones measured under hydrostatic compression and simulated uniaxial-strain drawdown differs by up to a factor of two, so hydrostatic values misrepresent production conditions.

Routine pore volume compressibility is measured under hydrostatic compression at constant (atmospheric) pore pressure, which does not reflect reservoir drawdown with constant overburden and zero lateral strain. Seven relatively unconsolidated sandstones were tested: three twin pairs (Group A) under both hydrostatic compression and simulated production, and four samples (Group B) under simulated production only. Drawdown ran from 4800 to 2000 psi with confining pressure servo-controlled to hold lateral strain constant. At equivalent effective mean stress, simulated-production compressibility was up to about twice the hydrostatic value (e.g., A-3 at 4200-2000 psi: 30.4 vs 22 ×10^-6 psi^-1; A-2: 28.9 vs 6). Samples A-1, A-2 and B-4 showed rising compressibility with drawdown, attributed to microfracturing and pore collapse driven by deviatoric stress. The authors conclude hydrostatic values should not be used directly for reservoir characterization and that compressibility depends on absolute pore pressure, not effective stress alone.

## EFFECTS OF CORING ON PETROPHYSICAL MEASUREMENTS. Rune M. Holt, IKU Petroleum Research and NTH Norwegian Institute of Technology, Trondheim, Norway release effects can be studied in a systematic way

- ID: SCA1994-07, year: 1994
- Authors: Rune M. Holt
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: core damage, stress release, synthetic sandstone, acoustic velocity, porosity reduction, compaction
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1994/SCA1994-07.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1994-07)

Synthetic sandstones formed under in situ stress and then unloaded and reloaded show that simulated coring permanently reduces porosity, sound velocities and compaction stiffness in weak sandstone.

Core damage from stress release, pore pressure release, temperature change and non-native fluids can permanently alter core properties, so core data may not represent the in situ rock. Weak synthetic sandstones (about 30% porosity, unconfined strength below 5 MPa) were cemented with sodium silicate and CO2 under 15 MPa axial and 7.5 MPa radial stress in a triaxial cell. They then went through unloading-reloading cycles simulating coring, with anisotropic or isotropic unloading, fast or slow. Anisotropic unloading left permanent porosity reductions of 0.5-1% units, which implies a 10-15% permeability reduction by a Kozeny-Carman estimate. P- and S-wave velocities fell 30-50% during unloading and stayed 5-15% lower after reloading. The reloaded rock was 50-100% softer than virgin rock at low stress, so core data would overestimate reservoir compaction.

## A Thermoporoelastic (T-P-E) Model Developed for Core Tripping Failure Analysis

- ID: SCA2017-026, year: 2017
- Authors: Rahman Ashena, Gerhard Thonhauser, Holger Ott, Vamegh Rasouli, Siroos Azizmohammadi, Michael Prohaska
- Method: theory or analytical model
- Keywords: core tripping, thermoporoelasticity, Griffith tensile failure, hydraulic diffusivity, induced stresses, tight gas core
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 1
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2017/SCA2017-026.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2017-026)

A step-wise thermoporoelastic model predicts tensile failure of tight cores during tripping; thermal stresses are minor, and hydraulic diffusivity and bottomhole depth control safe retrieval.

Tripping cores to surface drops confining pressure, pore pressure and temperature at the core boundary, and the lag in the core interior can induce tensile micro-fractures. The authors built an analytical thermoporoelastic model (Laplace transform solutions, implemented in Matlab) that treats tripping as discrete steps combined by superposition, and applies Griffith's tensile failure criterion to induced radial and hoop stresses. Benchmarking against a published Ansys-Fluent case (4-inch core, 2% porosity, 1502 m) showed good agreement, with the T-P-E model giving larger induced pore pressure because it includes mechanical and thermal effects. For a typical tight gas core (diffusivity 1e-8 m2/s, tensile strength ~2 MPa, 0.45 m/s tripping speed), thermal effects accounted for only about 6% of induced stresses. Failure was predicted for all bottomhole depths except 500 m, so the industry-suggested speed was judged unsuitable for very tight samples.

## Boundary Effect on Porosity Measurements and Its Resolution by Method and Mathematical Means

- ID: SCA1987-09, year: 1987
- Authors: Servet Unalmiser
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: boundary effect, caliper bulk volume, surface pore volume, Hassler core holder, porosity correction, overburden pressure
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1987/SCA1987-09.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1987-09)

Shows that caliper bulk volume creates false surface pore volume that biases porosity, proposes two mathematical corrections, and shows early overburden porosity loss partly reflects sleeve conformance.

Porosity from different methods differs because the plug surface boundary introduces distorted (false) pore volume. Six Berea and five Aramco sandstone plugs were measured six ways (helium Boyle's law in a Hassler holder, matrix cup, kerosene resaturation, caliper). The spread between lowest (direct PV/caliper BV) and highest (caliper BV minus matrix-cup GV) porosity averaged 1.2 porosity units (6.6%) for Berea and 4.4 units (16.8%) for Aramco. Two corrections were developed using surface pore volumes from mercury injection (0.025 cc Berea, 0.15 cc Aramco) and an assumed damaged surface depth tied to grain size. Full-diameter versus plug comparisons on Aramco limestones showed plug porosity 0.7–2.0 porosity units higher. A solid brass plug with machined channels lost all apparent pore volume by 600 psi confining pressure, showing that early porosity reduction under overburden is largely sleeve conformance, not compressibility. The authors recommend avoiding caliper bulk volume.

## SCA1990-07EURO

- ID: SCA1990-07EURO, year: 1990
- Authors: John A. Nieto, David P. Yale, Robert J. Evans
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: core compaction correction, pore volume compressibility, biaxial loading, uniaxial correction, creep, formation resistivity factor
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1990/SCA1990-07EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1990-07EURO)

Hydrostatic, slow-loading and biaxial tests on Central North Sea sandstones show compaction corrections below 0.95, with loading rate mattering more than stress state; uniaxial correction is questioned.

Core compaction correction in the North Sea is commonly taken as 0.95, derived from hydrostatic tests plus a uniaxial correction factor of 0.62 based on linear elasticity (Teeuw). The authors tested 24 Jurassic and Triassic sandstone plugs using three-cycle hydrostatic loading to 10,000 psi (8 hours), single-cycle loading at 750 psi/day (10 days), and biaxial loading with radial/axial ratios of 0.65-0.85 derived from fracture gradients. At 6000 psi effective stress, porosity reduction factors were about 0.89 (Jurassic) and 0.93 (Triassic) for three-cycle tests, and 0.87 and 0.90 for 10-day tests, so slow loading (creep) gave larger reduction. Biaxial results were similar to or within 1% of three-cycle results, indicating rate of loading matters more than stress regime. They argue the uniaxial correction is invalid because porosity-stress curves are nonlinear, and recommend measuring porosity at an in-situ-equivalent hydrostatic stress (about 0.856 of effective overburden) applied slowly.

## A Simple Check for the Presence of Solid Salt in the Pore Space of Gas Reservoirs by Comparing Porosity and Grain Density Changes Resulting from Salt Extraction

- ID: SCA1990-11EURO, year: 1990
- Authors: Werner H. Muck
- Method: data analysis or correlation
- Keywords: solid salt, salt extraction, Archimedes porosity, grain density, North German gas reservoirs, halite
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1990/SCA1990-11EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1990-11EURO)

Compares porosity and grain density before and after salt extraction to compute the density of removed material, distinguishing natural solid salt from drying or mud-filtrate artifacts in North German gas reservoirs.

North German gas reservoir cores often give porosities and grain densities that are too low when measured on uncleaned, oven-dried plugs, because of salt in the pore space. Porosity and grain density were measured by the Archimedes (isopropanol buoyancy) method before and after salt extraction, with repeatability of ±0.1 p.u. and reproducibility of ±0.2 p.u. The average density of the extracted material and an excess porosity term (accounting for 15% evaporation residue of saturated brine) classify samples into three types: no solid salt, natural solid salt (density 2.16 ± 0.2 g/cm3), or salt plugging pore throats. In a middle Bunter core, most of the 26 samples showed porosity rising from about 5% to 25% after extraction and extracted-material densities near 2.2 g/cm3, confirming halite. The error analysis shows interpretation becomes unreliable below a porosity change of 2 p.u. A routine screen of 200 wells flagged 15 as probably containing solid salt, found only in the middle Bunter and Zechstein. Only five of these had been noted in the original core reports. Plugs should be drilled with mineral oil if solid salt is indicated, since fresh water drilling raised permeability from 40 to 400 mD.

## Poroperm Evaluation on in Relation to Applied Stress States for a Gas Reservoir

- ID: SCA1990-13EURO, year: 1990
- Authors: Rodney A. Farquhar, Dianne E. Tompkins
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: stress-dependent permeability, Klinkenberg permeability, stress state, helium porosity, Rotliegendes sandstone, drill stem test permeability
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1990/SCA1990-13EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1990-13EURO)

Porosity and Klinkenberg permeability of North Sea Rotliegendes sandstones were measured under hydrostatic, biaxial, triaxial and uniaxial stress; stress-corrected permeability averages matched DST values better than conventional data.

Conventional core analysis is run at non-standard confining stresses, so the effect of stress state and rock type on porosity and permeability was tested. Plugs from Permian Rotliegendes lithic and sublithic arenites (aeolian, fluvial and lake margin facies) were loaded from 500 to 4000 psi in a purpose-built core holder, with helium porosity and Klinkenberg permeability measured at each step. Facies-based conversion factors gave pseudo-stress permeability averages closer to DST permeabilities than conventional core data in most intervals.

## Core Analysis to Calibrate Geophysical Interpretation

- ID: SCA1991-02EURO, year: 1991
- Authors: Dominique Marion, Bernard Zinszner
- Method: review or workflow
- Keywords: acoustic velocity, velocity-porosity relationship, sonic log calibration, pore geometry, clay content, velocity dispersion
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1991/1-SCA1991-02EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1991-02EURO)

Reviews how porosity, pore geometry, saturation, fluid type and clay affect core acoustic velocities, and shows how core data calibrate seismic and sonic interpretation.

The paper reviews how rock and fluid properties control acoustic velocity and how core measurements can calibrate geophysical interpretation. Core data show that velocity depends on porosity but also on pore geometry (vuggy dolomites have higher velocity at a given porosity), clay content (Han et al. regressions for Vp and Vs), stress, saturation and fluid type. Compressional velocity in tar sand can fall as much as 30% with heating versus 3-5% in water-saturated sand, supporting seismic monitoring of thermal EOR fronts. Applications include gas bright-spot discrimination using P and S waves, conversion of tomograms to porosity maps, and sonic log calibration, where upscaled core velocities agree with the sonic log within 3% in rapidly varying intervals. Core-derived velocity-porosity relations predicted reservoir porosity better than the time-average equation, which underestimated it. The authors caution that frequency dispersion and scale differences limit direct transfer of laboratory results to seismic and sonic scales.

## Acoustic Core Analysis in Reservoir Appraisal

- ID: SCA1991-11EURO, year: 1991
- Authors: W. Keith Hirsche, Zhijing Wang, George Sedgwick, Western Geophysical, U.K. Isleworth
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: acoustic velocity, carbonate cores, Gassmann equation, pore aspect ratio, effective confining pressure, Vp/Vs fluid substitution
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1991/1-SCA1991-11EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1991-11EURO)

Ultrasonic P and S velocities on over 100 Alberta carbonate cores show pressure, porosity and saturation effects, and a three-type pore-geometry classification explains variable fluid sensitivity.

The study addresses the non-unique link between seismic velocity and porosity, pressure and pore fluid in carbonates. Pulse-transmission velocities (500 kHz) were measured on more than 100 Alberta limestone and dolomite cores (porosity 0.02-0.21) at net confining pressures of 6.9-34.5 MPa, under gas, mineral oil, solvent and water saturation. Vp rose 2-35% in gas-saturated cores with pressure, and porosity-velocity correlation was -0.72 (gas) and -0.84 (oil). Gas-to-oil saturation raised Vp by an average 13%, 7% and 6% at 6.9, 20.7 and 34.5 MPa, while oil and water differed by under 3%. Gassmann predicted saturated velocities within about 2% on average, whereas the time-average equation under-predicted; Toksoz modelling attributed deviations to low-aspect-ratio pores. Cores were classified into Types 1-3 by saturation sensitivity (>10%, 5-10%, <5%), with Type 3 giving porosity correlations better than -0.9 and Vp/Vs averaging 1.85 in gas versus 1.97 in oil.

## Integration of Core and Downhole Acoustic Measurements - Shear and Compressional

- ID: SCA1991-12EURO, year: 1991
- Authors: John A. Nieto, David P. Yale
- Method: data analysis or correlation
- Keywords: shear wave velocity, dipole sonic log, Biot-Gassmann, Poisson's ratio, static vs dynamic moduli, Rotliegendes sandstone
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1991/1-SCA1991-12EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1991-12EURO)

Core P and S velocities on Rotliegendes sandstone agree with dipole sonic logs, giving porosity transforms; drained dynamic Poisson's ratio matches static, but other dynamic moduli do not.

The study integrates laboratory shear and compressional velocities on brine-saturated Rotliegendes sandstone plugs and whole cores (1 to 4 in. diameter, two wells) with Mobil SWAL dipole shear and LSAL compressional logs. Velocities were measured to 5000 psi confining stress (4000 psi effective), with a compaction porosity correction of 0.94 of ambient. Core and log velocities agree well (shear transit time 123.0 vs 124.5 µs/ft), and porosity-velocity transforms reproduce core porosity over both wells.
