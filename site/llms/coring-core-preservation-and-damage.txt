## Reservoir Petrophysics of Poorly Consolidated Rocksi. Well-Site Procedures and Laboratory Methods

- ID: SCA1987-04, year: 1987
- Authors: A. E. Worthington, J. Gidman, G. H. Newman, La Habra
- Method: measurement technique or apparatus
- Keywords: poorly consolidated sandstone, well-site core handling, resin stabilization, plunge cutting, restored stress, formation factor
- Cited by 7 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1987/SCA1987-04.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1987-04)

Describes well-site stabilization with resin, chilling and plunge-cut plugs, plus unjacketed at-stress lab measurements, for poorly consolidated cores, giving better preservation than freezing and jacketing.

Freezing and jacketing of poorly consolidated cores disrupts bedding contacts and causes grain rearrangement and crushing. The authors describe careful barrel and liner handling, filling the core-liner annulus with a self-polymerizing isocyanate-polyol resin, ice-temperature storage, top slabbing, and plunge cutting of plugs. Plugs go directly into a Hassler-type pressure vessel with a KalRez sleeve at net effective stress. There they are cleaned by solvent flow, miscibly saturated with brine, and measured for brine permeability, formation factor, resistivity index, air permeability, pore volume and grain density. Applied to over 1400 ft of Inglewood Field core with over 94% recovery, the method preserved sand-shale contacts, and cleaning took days rather than weeks. Fines migrated substantially when air displaced NaCl brine, but only slightly with KCl/CaCl2 brine.

## New Coring System Reduces Filtrate Invasion

- ID: SCA1990-03EURO, year: 1990
- Authors: Gordon A. Tibbitts, M.G. Reed, M. McCarter, Eastman Christensen Co
- Method: field or reservoir case study
- Keywords: filtrate invasion, low invasion core bit, PDC cutters, bromide tracer, spurt loss, Dean-Stark saturation
- Cited by 4 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1990/SCA1990-03EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1990-03EURO)

A low-invasion PDC core bit, piloted shoe and bland low-spurt-loss mud kept filtrate within about 0.5 inch of the core surface in Elk Hills Stevens sandstone.

Coring fluid filtrate invasion compromises measured saturations, wettability and permeability. The authors describe a low invasion system with a sparse-cutter PDC bit with shaped gage cutters, a nested pilot core shoe, high penetration rates and a bland low-spurt-loss water-base mud. It was field tested in the Stevens sand at Elk Hills, cutting 571 ft of 4-inch core with 96.3% recovery at an average 68.6 ft/hr, with sandstone rates of about 150-310 ft/hr versus about 5 ft/hr for conventional diamond bits. Bromide and iodide tracers and Dean-Stark saturations on 38 dissected depths showed average invasion of about 0.25 inch, with little beyond 0.5 inch, and usable uninvaded centers at every depth. Invasion was not significantly related to brine permeability (0.01-45 md), though the outer half-inch was partially flushed of oil.

## Reservoir Water Saturation Measured on Cores; Case Histories and Recommendations

- ID: SCA1999-06, year: 1999
- Authors: J.K. Ringen, C. Halvorsen, K.A. Lehne, H. Rueslaatten, H. Holand
- Method: field or reservoir case study
- Keywords: Dean-Stark extraction, mud filtrate invasion, tritium tracer, water saturation, Archie saturation exponent, oil-based mud coring
- Cited by 3 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1999/SCA1999-06.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1999-06)

Reviews methods and six North Sea case histories for obtaining reservoir water saturation from cores, showing tracer-doped mud and rig-site plugging are needed with water-based mud.

Core water saturation is often compromised by mud filtrate invasion, so the paper sets out a workflow covering planning, tracer doping (tritium or deuterium), low-invasion coring, rig-site plugging, Dean-Stark extraction and corrections. With water-based mud, invasion can be large and increases at the top of each core and in higher permeability. Plugs drilled at the rig site showed more tracer than those drilled a week later in the lab. With oil-based mud, tracer showed little or no water invasion into the core centre. Six Statoil field cases show core Sw verifying logs, adjusting Archie n (e.g. 2.04 to 2.28), evaluating wettability effects (n of about 2.13 to 2.90), and handling thin-bed and microporous formations. Tritium costs about US$1000 per well versus about US$10,000 for D2O.

## Application of Low Invasion Coring and Outcrop Studies to Reservoir Development Planning for the Villano Field

- ID: SCA1999-02, year: 1999
- Authors: J. J. Rathmell, Laura K. Atkins, James G. Kralik
- Method: field or reservoir case study
- Keywords: low invasion coring, bromide tracer filtrate, connate water saturation, steady-state relative permeability, Archie saturation exponent, stochastic shale modeling
- Cited by 3 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1999/SCA1999-02.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1999-02)

Low invasion water-based-mud coring, steady-state relative permeability, and outcrop-based shale modeling in the Villano Field gave reliable water saturation, salinity gradient, and a development plan.

Villano 2 logs gave connate water saturation near 30% versus about 17% from mercury injection, so Villano 3 was cored with water-based mud using a bromide tracer for low invasion. Filtrate averaged about 5% pv in plugs above 10 md, and about 86% of horizontal plugs had under 10% pv filtrate. Corrected core data revealed a chloride gradient from 36,000 ppm at the top to 2600 ppm at the water-oil contact. With native-state Archie n varying with saturation, log-derived Sw matched core (mean 9.84% vs 9.96% pv). Unsteady-state waterflood relative permeability was unreliable because of viscous instability at a viscosity ratio of 40, whereas steady-state and centrifuge data were consistent (Sorw 0.187). Outcrop and core data conditioned a stochastic shale generator for a simulation that supported a plan of five high-angle wells at a 40,000 STBD plateau.

## The Preservation or Removal of Solid Bituminous Material, as Part of the Core Analysis Programme on the Elgin-Franklin Field

- ID: SCA2001-48, year: 2001
- Authors: Clive Cornwall
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: pyrobitumen, core cleaning, Soxhlet extraction, grain density, HPHT gas condensate, thin section point counting
- Cited by 2 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2001/SCA2001-48.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2001-48)

Benign versus harsh cleaning and drying of Elgin-Franklin pyrobitumen-bearing sandstone plugs produced only small rises in porosity, permeability and grain density, near measurement repeatability, so routine preparation was judged acceptable.

Elgin-Franklin HPHT gas condensate sandstones contain solid pyrobitumen and kerogen, and the concern was that standard cleaning could remove them and artificially raise permeability, porosity and grain density. Plug pairs from two 'black core' locations were cleaned and dried sequentially with a benign regime (isopropanol/methanol, constant immersion, 60°C humidity-controlled oven) and a harsh regime (toluene/methanol Soxhlet, 95°C oven). Between cycles, porosity rose by about 0.5-0.6 porosity percent, grain density by about 0.01 g/cc, and permeability by up to about 1 mD on ~19 mD samples. Point-count bitumen content fell from 17.3% to 13.3%, but this is within the semi-quantitative repeatability of 200-point counts. The changes were near the measurement repeatability, so the evidence was insufficient to show that standard preparation harms data quality. A benign-harsh control was nonetheless applied to development cores, with average variations of about 1% in permeability and 0.45-0.56 porosity percent across 628 and 37 control plugs in two wells.

## Accuracy and Reliability of Electronic Instrument Core Orientation Surveys

- ID: SCA1990-28, year: 1990
- Authors: WILLIAM E. HENRY
- Method: field or reservoir case study
- Keywords: core orientation, electronic survey instrument, paleomagnetic orientation, tool vibration, master orientation line, quality control
- Cited by 2 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1990/SCA1990-28.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1990-28)

Case studies from two Texas wells show electronic core orientation surveys suffer from vibration, torque, time-depth and setup errors; quality control procedures and paleomagnetic checks are recommended.

Electronic survey instrument (E.S.I.) core orientation replaces multishot photography but is prone to errors from tool setup, vibration, torque, data editing and core handling. In a west Texas Ellenburger well (SFBU 4-62), eight oriented cores were taken under foam drilling with severe vibration. Multiple reports for the same core differed by up to 170°, subjective editing hid scatter of up to 173° in raw shots, and one core was misoriented by about 144° because of incorrect time-depth correlation. Paleomagnetic orientation exposed these errors, and corrected E.S.I. reports then agreed within about ±15° (±5° for the time-corrected core). A south Texas well showed alignment errors of about 20° from a loose telescope, bent barrel and miscalibrated cross-hair, fixed with a rifle sight and level calibration verified to ±1°. The paper gives a checklist of quality control procedures including stationary readings, stopwatch timing, a Master Orientation Line on core, and routine paleomagnetic checks.

## Application of NMR- and CT-Analytical Methods to Assess the Formation Damage Potential of Drilling Fluids

- ID: SCA1998-13, year: 1998
- Authors: C.H. van der Zwaag, F. Stallmach, Jan Erik Hanssen
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: formation damage, NMR relaxation time, NMR imaging, drilling fluid additives, return permeability, X-ray CT
- Cited by 3 papers in the corpus, cites 2
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1998/SCA1998-13.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1998-13)

NMR relaxometry, NMR imaging and CT on oil-saturated Berea show PAC and xanthan cause lasting damage while polyglycol is largely removed by backflooding.

The study assesses formation damage from three water-based mud additives (polyanionic cellulose, xanthan, polyglycol) in oil-saturated hollow-cylinder Berea sandstone (ka about 80 md). Samples were flooded at 80°C with additive solutions (50 PV for PAC and glycol, 9 PV for xanthan) and backflooded with crude oil. NMR T2 distributions, NMR imaging with radial relaxation-time-weighted porosity profiles, and CT were run at each saturation state. Return oil permeability was 39% for PAC, 77% for glycol and 44% for xanthan. PAC accumulated near the inflow hole and persisted after backflooding, xanthan displaced oil evenly but was poorly back-produced, and glycol effects were temporary. CT showed insignificant contrast between fluid states because brine and oil have similar density, whereas NMR detected the changes.

## Increasing the Value of Core Analysis

- ID: SCA1991-04EURO, year: 1991
- Authors: Peter R. Whattler
- Method: review or workflow
- Keywords: core analysis reporting, plug history sheet, core continuity and depth shifting, Klinkenberg permeability, wellsite core handling, special core analysis
- Cited by 2 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1991/1-SCA1991-04EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1991-04EURO)

Describes Enterprise Oil's contractual work schedules and report structures that make core contractors record wellsite, laboratory and experimental observations, with examples showing practical benefits.

Historical core analysis reports are often bare data tables with a covering letter, which limits later reuse and can mislead inexperienced users. Enterprise Oil formalised work schedules, backed by contract penalty clauses, covering coring, wellsite core catching, laboratory core fitting, plugging, routine analysis, photography, preservation and reporting. Routine analysis includes separate horizontal and vertical plugs, Hassler permeability at 200 and 400 psi, Klinkenberg and overburden tests. Reports follow a 20-section routine structure and a special core analysis structure that includes plug history sheets, a reference volume of worksheets and calibrations, and data on magnetic media. Examples show benefits: a wellsite technician's report explained poor core recovery, core break continuity coding eased depth matching, and a saw-blade wear observation led to a toothed core head with savings of about $6 x 15k per well.

## The Effects of Freezing on Tracer Distribution in Cores

- ID: SCA1992-12EURO, year: 1992
- Authors: June Gidman, Frank J. Conner
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: core freezing, mud filtrate tracer, fluid migration, oil saturation, sandpack, liquid nitrogen
- Cited by 3 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1992/1-SCA1992-12EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1992-12EURO)

Freezing brine-saturated cores and sandpacks in dry ice or liquid nitrogen redistributes salt tracers, so frozen-core tracer analysis misjudges mud filtrate invasion; subsample at the wellsite.

Tracers added to coring mud are used to estimate filtrate invasion and correct oil saturation, and cores are normally frozen on the assumption that this immobilizes fluids. The authors saturated 4-inch Berea, kaolinitic and Boise sandstone cores and Ottawa sandpacks (coarse, fine, layered) with KCl or KBr brines, froze them in dry ice or liquid nitrogen, stored them frozen, and subsampled them in a freezer for K+ or Cl- analysis. In every case tracer migrated inward and upward. Dry-ice sandpacks showed center concentrations of about 278% of original K+, with surface ice from 50% to over 1000% of original. Cores showed smaller shifts (about 20% in the kaolinitic core). Liquid nitrogen gave only about 10% deviation but fractured the Boise core. They conclude that tracers are not immobilized by freezing and recommend sampling and subsampling at the wellsite.

## Core Sample Preparation – an Insight in to New Procedures

- ID: SCA2004-50, year: 2004
- Authors: M. Byrne, I. Patey
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: acetate peel, rock flour, Continuous Immersion Soxhlet cleaning, fibrous illite, core cleaning and drying, SEM
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 1
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2004/SCA2004-50.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2004-50)

Proposes an acetate peel to remove rock flour from plug end faces and a continuous immersion Soxhlet cleaning method that avoids illite collapse seen with hot Soxhlet and hot drying.

Plug cutting, cleaning and drying can alter rock properties, so the authors propose two new preparation procedures. An acetone-softened acetate peel removes rock flour from plug end faces; SEM shows brushing pushes fines into pores while the peel leaves them open. Air permeability rose slightly after peeling (fine sandstone 7.18 to 7.56 mD, chalk 1.83 to 1.92 mD). A Continuous Immersion Soxhlet (CIS) cleaner keeps samples immersed in warm solvent, followed by 60°C drying. Conventional hot Soxhlet with 120°C drying collapsed fibrous illite in samples with 0.8–4.3% illite and raised air permeability (e.g. 60.3 vs 48.4 mD, 13.9 vs 10.2 mD), with little change in grain density and small porosity increases.

## Laboratory Assessment of the Efficiency of Core Preservation Techniques

- ID: SCA2007_58, year: 2007
- Authors: Jean-Valery Garcia, Kirk Petrophysics, Andrew Hurst, Colin Taylor
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: core preservation, wax dipping, Lithotarge foam, dehydration weight loss, pore volume loss
- Cited by 2 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2007/SCA2007_58.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2007_58)

Weight-loss dehydration tests on 36 plugs compare three commercial waxes with Lithotarge foam; waxes lose under 0.5% pore volume over 38 weeks, foam about 2.8%.

Core preservation aims to keep native fluids and their distribution until later analysis. Nine sets of four adjacent 1.5-inch clastic and carbonate plugs (0.07 to ~2000 mD gas permeability) were cleaned, dried and resaturated, then preserved with one of three commercial waxes (wrapped in Saran/aluminium) or Lithotarge closed-cell foam. Weight loss was tracked for 38 weeks, expressed as percent of pore volume. Wax-dipped samples lost under 0.5% of pore volume at both 6 and 38 weeks, with no clear differences between the waxes. Lithotarge lost about 1.7% at 6 weeks and 2.8% at 38 weeks, but performed comparably to waxes up to about 60 days. Loss was highest in the lowest-porosity samples with little trend against permeability; the aluminium/Saran wrap is probably the main barrier in the wax method.

## Recent Experience with Unconsolidated Core Analysis

- ID: SCA2007_41, year: 2007
- Authors: Richard Rosen, Bill Mickelson, Jason Fry, George Hill, Bob Knabe, Munir Sharf-Aldin, Meta Rock
- Method: measurement technique or apparatus
- Keywords: unconsolidated sand, core freezing, bulk volume strain, stressed mercury capillary pressure, resistivity index, sample damage
- Cited by 2 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2007/SCA2007_41.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2007_41)

Describes Shell's protocols and new equipment for handling, freezing, plugging and measuring unconsolidated cores, using bulk volume strain to flag sample damage and enabling 30,000 psi stressed mercury capillary pressure.

Unconsolidated sand is very difficult to analyze, so every step from coring to measurement must limit disturbance. The paper describes Shell's practice of keeping core frozen with dry ice, stabilizing it in cardboard tubes with foam, and drilling plugs with liquid nitrogen. Plugs are Teflon-sleeved with sintered frits and loaded into stress cells within 30 seconds, with bulk volume strain gauges to monitor damage. Benchmark undamaged samples show strains of 1-5%, while strains above 10% (e.g. a 40% porosity plug with CT-visible dilation) give unreasonable results. New equipment includes high-precision syringe pumps, 16-bit transducers, a mercury capillary pressure apparatus to 30,000 psi with Boyle's law pore volume and strain measurement, and a hybrid continuous-injection/equilibrium resistivity index and crude-brine capillary pressure apparatus.

## SCAL : An Indispensable Step for Formation Damage Evaluation

- ID: SCA2000-15, year: 2000
- Authors: D.G. Longeron
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: formation damage, dynamic mud filtration, return permeability, filter cake breakers, invert emulsion mud, long core tests
- Cited by 2 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2000/SCA2000-15.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2000-15)

Dynamic mud leak-off tests on 40 cm sandstone cores compare 8 muds and cleanup breakers; invert emulsion muds damage less, and IEM breakers worsen damage.

The paper presents an IFP laboratory methodology to simulate drilling mud invasion on long cores (5 cm diameter, up to 40 cm) with dynamic filtration, pressure taps along the core and oil backflow to measure return permeability. Study 1 tested five water-based and three invert emulsion muds on high-permeability Vosges sandstone. WBM gave global return permeabilities of 11-39% with severe damage (8-22%) in the first 5 cm, partly from trapped filtrate. IEM gave 68-94%, with local return permeabilities above 100% at 10-30 cm, possibly from reduced connate water; surfactants cut IFT by a factor of up to 45. Study 2 on Vosges and Berea cores showed WBM breakers (NaOCl, enzyme) raised ultimate return permeability by 10-26%, whereas IEM breakers (EGMBE, emulsified acid, surfactants) added damage of 6-48% IRP and 7-30% URP, so natural cleanup is recommended for IEM.

## Effect of Glauconite on Petrophysical Properties as Revealed by Core Analysis

- ID: SCA2003-32, year: 2003
- Authors: Wibeke Hammervold Thomas, Jon Knut Ringen, Sten Ola Rasch
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: glauconite, Dean-Stark water saturation, resistivity index, saturation exponent, ESEM, micro-porosity
- Cited by 2 papers in the corpus, cites 1
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2003/SCA2003-32.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2003-32)

Special core analysis of a North Sea glauconitic sandstone shows water-filled, water-wet glauconite pellets explain high Dean-Stark water saturations, nonlinear resistivity index behavior and humidity-dependent porosity.

Logs and Dean-Stark data disagreed on water saturation in a North Sea sandstone (porosity ~31-32%, permeability 100-200 mD). Log estimates were 20-40% while Dean-Stark gave 40-70%, even with traced oil-based mud, and well tests produced only oil. Petrography and ESEM showed 18-30% glauconite pellets with ~40% internal porosity that were water-filled and water-wet. Capillary pressure and resistivity tests gave very high irreducible water saturation and concave-upward RI vs Sw curves, opposite to the clay effect; n was 3.0 by endpoint versus 2.7 by regression. Humidity-controlled drying lowered measured porosity by about 1.5 p.u. Using an effective pore system made RI curves closer to linear and gave normal Sorw values (16-17% versus 7-12% total).

## Improved Method for Extraction and Quantification of Hydrocarbon Content of Sponge Core Liners

- ID: SCA1990-19, year: 1990
- Authors: R. DiFoggio, C. L. Calkin, W. E. Ellington, G. G. Setser
- Method: measurement technique or apparatus
- Keywords: sponge core, Freon-11 extraction, Soxhlet solvent disperser, oil saturation, gas chromatography residual solvent, asphaltene precipitation
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1990/SCA1990-19.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1990-19)

Freon-11 Soxhlet extraction of sponge core liners, partial distillation and GC residual-solvent correction accurately recover oil volumes for crudes of 15-40 API gravity.

Sponge core oil quantification was unreliable in a 1985 blind test of service company methods, showing random and systematic errors. The authors selected Freon-11 as extraction solvent because it does not swell polyurethane, dissolves crude well with little asphaltene precipitation, and boils at 23.8°C. They designed Soxhlet extractors for one-foot sponge sections in aluminum liners, with sintered stainless steel solvent dispersers and a chilled 5-bulb Allihn condenser, refluxing about one pore volume per hour for 48 hours. Extracts were partially distilled to 65°C vapor temperature, removing about 85% of the Freon, and residual Freon was measured by GC and subtracted, with centrifugation and KOH used to remove sponge material. Tests on synthetic samples with four oils showed good agreement between injected and measured volumes, and over 3000 feet of sponge core have been analyzed, with good agreement with log saturations.

- ID: SCA1991-08EURO, year: 1991
- Authors: Diana K.T. Hansen, Milton B. Enderlin, Fort Worth
- Method: field or reservoir case study
- Keywords: oriented sidewall cores, anisotropy of magnetic susceptibility, borehole televiewer, directional permeability, skin damage, oriented thin sections
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 1
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/1991/1-SCA1991-08EURO.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA1991-08EURO)

Shows how rotary-drilled sidewall cores can be oriented from tool marks and televiewer images, enabling AMS, oriented thin sections, directional permeability and skin damage studies.

Whole core is often unavailable, so the authors examine whether rotary-drilled sidewall cores can be oriented and used for directional petrophysical and sedimentologic work. In a nine-well Devonian sandstone study of 195 cores, 86% showed increased permeability after trimming to the formation end, though trimming and ultrasonic cleaning may contribute. The authors describe these as preliminary studies needing further validation.

## Avoiding Routine Core Analysis Plug Damage by Proper Evaluation of Core Gamma-Ray, Core Description and Wellsite Core Sampling

- ID: SCA2018-047, year: 2018
- Authors: Norbert Schleifer, Emmanuel Kesse, George Lawrence, Wintershall Holding, Wintershall Norge, DEA Deutsche Erdöl AG
- Method: field or reservoir case study
- Keywords: plug cleaning damage, Soxhlet extraction, core gamma-ray, Dean Stark, Hot Shot plugs, routine core analysis
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 1
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2018/SCA2018-047.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2018-047)

Two German oilfield cases show 49% and 60% of RCA plugs fractured during cleaning, concentrated in high-gamma, muddy zones identifiable beforehand, so milder selective cleaning is recommended.

Routine core analysis plugs from two German wells (Dogger sandstone and Malm carbonate) were damaged during cleaning: 43 of 88 plugs in well M (mild chloroform/methanol immersion) and 128 of 212 in well S (hot Soxhlet with methanol then toluene). The authors compared damaged plug locations with total core gamma-ray, quick-look core description, and wellsite Dean Stark and Hot Shot plugs. Damage correlated with high gamma-ray zones (above about 120 API in well M) and with mudstone, argillaceous sandstone, distal mouth bar and pro-delta facies, as well as some wackestone. Additional well S plugs cleaned by immersion showed no damage. Oil saturation effects could not be evaluated because only three Dean Stark data points lay in damaged zones. They recommend using these data to flag fragile zones and apply milder or cold cleaning there, avoiding biased data and loss of SCAL backup plugs.

## Maximizing the Impact of Routine Core Analysis on Special Core and Log Analysis: Unconsolidated Sandstones

- ID: SCA2001-07, year: 2001
- Authors: Ted Griffin, John Shafer
- Method: field or reservoir case study
- Keywords: unconsolidated sandstone, particle size distribution, CT scanning, core-to-log depth shift, net-to-gross, probe permeability
- Cited by 1 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2001/SCA2001-07.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2001-07)

Shows how wellsite measurements, CT, particle size data, probe permeability and core photo analysis on frozen unconsolidated sandstone core support early log analysis, net-to-gross estimates and SCAL plug selection.

Unconsolidated, interbedded oil sandstone was cored with oil-based mud in three 90-ft cores, stabilized by freezing in dry ice, and analyzed with a high-density routine program (249 statistical plugs, 411 archived). ## Sample Preparation for Unconventional Analysis: A Case Against Solvent Extraction

- ID: SCA2014-002, year: 2014
- Authors: Jon Burger, Doug McCarty, Russ Peacher, Timothy Fischer
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: Dean-Stark extraction, retort porosity, shale organic matter, thermogravimetric analysis, Rock-Eval, subcritical nitrogen adsorption
- Cited by 2 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2014/SCA2014-002.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2014-002)

Dean-Stark solvent extraction raises shale porosity by up to 2 PU versus retort because it removes solid organic matter, not clay structural water.

Dean-Stark (solvent) and retort preparation give different porosities in unconventional rocks, typically 1-2 PU in 4-8 PU systems. A round robin on equivalent quarter core sections sent to three vendors showed Dean-Stark porosity up to 2 PU above retort, while total evolved water volumes were nearly identical. XRD before and after Dean-Stark showed no clay dehydroxylation peak shifts, ruling out structural water loss. Rock-Eval showed Dean-Stark removed most S1 and about half of S2, with TOC reduced by 15%. TGA and N2 adsorption showed Dean-Stark removes organic matter in the 400-600°C range and opens more pores than a 200°C thermal extraction. The authors conclude solvent extraction alters organic-rich pore networks and overestimates porosity, and recommend retort or thermal extraction.

- Authors: B. Marchand, C. A. Davy, F. Agostini, F. Skoczylas, A. Lange, L. Jeannin, P. Egermann
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: chemical consolidation, sand production, fluidization test, polyacrylamide binder, gas storage, gas permeability
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2018/SCA2018-041.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2018-041)

Novel chemical binders injected into compacted sand analogues strengthen them like PAM, resist water injection better, and binders E and F preserve gas permeability.

Sand production in unconsolidated gas-storage reservoirs can be limited by chemical consolidation, but existing binders such as PAM reduce permeability and lose strength with water. Compacted standard siliceous sand tubes (10 mm diameter, porosity 26.5-28.5%) were used as analogues of the weakest in situ sandstones (porosity 15.5-28.7%), checked by porosity and MIP/micro-CT pore size distributions. Seven new binders (SB, A-F) were injected, flushed with air and matured under three schemes (m1-m3, 20 or 70 °C), then tested by argon fluidization up to 15 ln/min or 0.5 MPa, with and without water injection. Binders A-D gave Pmax 1.5-3.1 times that of PAM and survived water injection, but gas permeability fell 1.3-2.2 times below PAM. Binders E and F gave Pmax similar to PAM without permeability loss and kept strength after water injection at 70 °C, but failed after 20 °C maturation; PAM and SB failed after water injection.

## Formation Damage in the Inter-Well Zones: Experiments and Advanced Analytics

- ID: SCA2018-043, year: 2018
- Authors: Denis Orlov, Dmitry Koroteev
- Method: data analysis or correlation
- Keywords: self-colmatation, fines migration, formation damage, machine learning, XGBoost, feature importance
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2018/SCA2018-043.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2018-043)

Predicts self-colmatation (fines-migration permeability damage) in Vendian sandstones from rock properties using cross-plot correlations and machine learning; machine learning gave higher R2 and consistent feature rankings.

Self-colmatation, the permeability loss caused by entrainment of in-situ fines during clean water injection, was studied in Lower Vendian sandstones of the Nepa-Botuoba Arch. Twenty-two single-phase water floods at residual oil saturation (porosity 6-21%, permeability 10-1650 mD) gave permeability reductions of 1.02 to 4.76 times after 3.5-20 pore volumes. The curves were fitted with a three-parameter model (α, n, Rmin), which was then related to 16-17 rock and flooding features by manual cross-plots and by machine learning, with missing data filled by neural networks. Machine learning models reached R2 of 0.68 (α, XGBoost), 0.61 (n, Lasso) and 0.44 (Rmin, Gradient Boosting), above the manual averages of 0.62, 0.36 and 0.33. Permeability, porosity, microcline content and the fractions of 6-12 µm and >30 µm pore throats were the dominant features, and the two approaches largely agreed. The small dataset with missing values limits the reliability of these R2 values.

## Integrated workflow for rotary sidewall cores orientation: best practices and examples from planning to execution

- ID: SCA2021-027, year: 2021
- Authors: Giorgio Volonté, Antonella Bersani, Roberto Berto, Riccardo Cerri, Fabio Pinelli
- Method: review or workflow
- Keywords: rotary sidewall cores, core orientation, X-ray CT unrolled image, borehole image logs, bedding dip matching
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 1
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2021/SCA2021-027.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2021-027)

Presents a workflow matching X-ray CT unrolled images of rotary sidewall cores to borehole image logs to reconstruct in situ orientation, with handling requirements and two field examples.

Rotary sidewall cores are increasingly used instead of bottom hole cores, but their in situ orientation is unknown, which limits geomechanical, permeability anisotropy, fracture and sedimentological studies. The paper sets minimum requirements for coring planning (before and after image logs, tool procedures), wellsite handling, marking, photography and CT scanning. It introduces the Eni SideWall Core Goniometry (e-SWCG) procedure: a 360° unrolled CT image (about 250 µm resolution) is loaded in dip-analysis software as an image log and rotated by trial and error until a geological feature matches the sinusoid picked on the log image. The method fixes the vertical direction uniquely, leaving a 180° azimuth/high-low side ambiguity that needs extra information such as post-coring images, tool taxi azimuth or wellbore end identification. In a carbonate case with all variables unknown, a match was reached after testing four base cases and a further 25° rotation. In a clastic case with tool taxi azimuth, a 10° lateral shift gave the match.

## Laboratory Measurement of Total Porosity and Fluid Saturations for Unconventional Tight Rocks: Methodologies, Challenges, and Comparison

- Authors: Min Cheng, Rayvan Watson, Alberto Latuff, Aaron Rodriguez, Peter Kaufman, Joshua Miller, Rafael A. Mendoza, Elizabeth Krukowski, Robert Cole
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: crushed rock analysis, closed retort, NMR T2 cutoff, T1T2 maps, Bakken, total porosity
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 4
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2023/SCA2023-021.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2023-021)

Compares CRA, 300C closed retort and NMR porosity and saturations on 15 Bakken cores, and derives NMR T2 cutoffs from retort free/bound fluid separation.

Total porosity and fluid saturations in tight unconventional rocks differ between crushed rock analysis, retort and NMR because of differing assumptions and uncertainties. Fifteen Bakken samples (Upper/Lower Bakken Shale, Middle Bakken, Three Forks) were tested with NMR (Te 0.1 and 0.2 ms, T1T2 maps) and 105/300C closed retort, and compared with legacy CRA/RCA, with XRD, TOC and MICP support. In shales, CRA porosity was about 31% higher than retort and 25% higher than NMR, likely from solvent removal of bitumen; NMR in non-shales was about 14% above RCA and 20% above retort. Crushing lost about 3.5% of liquid, and the 300 kppm brine required a supersaturated density/HI extrapolation (1.23 g/ml, HI 0.86). Combining retort free/bound water with NMR gave T2 cutoffs averaging 0.16 ms for shales, 0.91 ms for Middle Bakken and 4.59 ms for Three Forks.

## Core preservation packaging materials and techniques, their advantages and limitations

- ID: SCA2023-036, year: 2023
- Authors: Douglas Boyd, Mohamed Al Tamimi, Hesham Shebl
- Method: review or workflow
- Keywords: core preservation, laminate bags, wax and wrap, oxygen scavenger, water loss, wettability preservation
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 3
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2023/SCA2023-036.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2023-036)

Reviews core preservation materials (cling film, foil, wax, laminate bags, immersion) and storage, concluding laminates beat wrap-and-wax and suggesting a 2-3 year preservation limit.

The paper reviews materials and techniques for preserving cores after reaching surface, drawing on published studies (Hunt and Cobb 1988, Auman 1989, Wunderlich 1991, Garcia 2007, Rousselle 2014) and ADNOC experience. Cling films, especially post-2004 LDPE Saran Wrap, have high oxygen permeability and can swell in crude. Aluminum foil leaks at folds and corrodes in brine or at extreme pH, and waxes do not stop oxygen ingress or water loss. Wax-and-wrap samples lost 50-80% of water in two years versus 2-5% for laminate bags, and ADNOC whole cores were dry after five years. Laminate bags are recommended with vacuum, inert gas flush, oxygen scavenger and humidification, while oil immersion suits shale. Storage at 4-10 °C and 45-55% RH is advised and 2-3 years is offered as a preservation limit.

## Pre-operational drilling fluid evaluation of oil- and water-based drilling muds considering the impact on core analysis. A case study from the Upper Jurassic in the Northern North Sea

- ID: SCA2024_1060, year: 2024
- Authors: Norbert Schleifer, Jörn Schünemann, Oliver Czuprat, Ricardo Leo Marques Rouxinol, Arild Fjogstad
- Method: laboratory experiment
- Keywords: drilling mud contamination, wettability alteration, spontaneous imbibition, clay swelling, Kirby sandstone, XRF and μ-CT
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2024/SCA2024_1060.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2024_1060)

Three drilling muds (one OBM, two WBM) were tested on Kirby sandstone and cuttings for clay swelling, wettability alteration and formation damage; the OBM was selected despite greatest wettability change.

Core analysts are rarely involved in drilling fluid selection, yet muds can alter core properties. Two water-based and one synthetic oil-based mud were tested on Upper Jurassic cuttings (clay swelling) and on Kirby sandstone plugs (about 20% porosity, ~30 and ~40 mD) at Swi, after 24 h ultracentrifuge exposure at 50°C. The OBM limited clay swelling to under 4.5%, versus 9.7–18.6% for the WBMs and 20–31% for tap water. Flow-through cleaning with 5 PV oil removed OBM almost completely (mass change under 0.54 g), but left about 1 g (#2 WBM) and over 2 g (#3 WBM). Spontaneous imbibition showed all muds reduced water-wetness, with the OBM largest (Sw increase below 10%, versus above 20% for dummies); μ-CT and XRF detected barite on the outer surface of #2 WBM plugs and bromide from #3 WBM. Pre- versus post-test porosity and permeability dropped mainly for the WBM plugs, and the OBM was chosen for drilling based on overall performance and traceability.

## First Offshore Deployment of Large-Diameter Pressurised Coring Technology in a Mature Waterflooded Field

- ID: SCA2026-039, year: 2026
- Authors: Dave Saucier, Pavel Gramin, Rufat Guliyev, Murad Murtuzaliyev, Kevan Sincock, Tobias Rothenwänder, David Wunsch, Erik Anders, Ludovic Delmar
- Method: field or reservoir case study
- Keywords: pressure coring, remaining oil saturation, Dean-Stark extraction, mud filtrate tracer, waterflood, log calibration
- Cited by 0 papers in the corpus, cites 0
- [PDF](https://www.jgmaas.com/SCA/2026/SCA2026-039.pdf) · [On the site](https://mralbu.github.io/sca-papers/#SCA2026-039)

Case history of the first offshore large-diameter pressure coring in a waterflooded field: six full-length runs, validated remaining-oil saturations agreeing with log-derived values despite two seal failures.

Quantifying remaining oil behind the waterflood front in a mature offshore field is difficult with conventional coring, so a drillpipe-conveyed large-diameter pressure corer (LPC) was deployed from a PDQ platform in a complex 3D S-shaped sidetrack drilled with oil-based mud. Six 3 m runs cut 17.8 m and recovered 18.21 m (102%), comprising three mudstones and three sandstones; four runs held pressure to surface, while two sandstone runs had seal problems (one partial, one unpressurised). Cores were de-pressured at the wellsite with gas sampling and GOR measurement, then Dean–Stark extracted and corrected for mud filtrate using 1-BN oil tracer and for shrinkage. Annulus versus return-line tracer differed by only 0.7% (below 1% uncertainty), indicating negligible oil loss to the annulus. Core oil saturations agreed with log-derived values from Archie inputs and resistivity-while-drilling, and a wellsite Sor estimate matched laboratory results within about three saturation units for fully pressurised cores. Lessons included seal and core-catcher redesign, depth control, and hole cleaning.
